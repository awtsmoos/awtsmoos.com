# When the Runners Lowered Their Flags

Giv’at Or kept six signal poles between the upper spring and the market. A blue flag meant open the channel. Red meant close it. White meant a messenger was carrying an order that could not be trusted to color alone. Uri son of Haggai ran those orders faster than any man in town, down stone stairs polished by generations of sandals.

On the morning the south cistern cracked, every flag turned red.

Avram son of Netanel had not ordered a general closure. The spring steward had authorized only a pressure reduction, but Eliakim’s foreman at the third gate feared liability and closed his section completely. The second gate saw the red flag below and closed in response. Within minutes the lower quarter had no water at all.

Leah sent Uri uphill with a strip of linen tied around his wrist. Three fever patients needed washing. Mattan sent a clay shard marked with the pressure figures. The old channel could safely carry half-flow.

Avram read both messages in the spring house. His first instinct was administrative anger: no gatekeeper should have exceeded an order. His second was more dangerous. He saw that the system’s caution would always multiply downward. Every station could transmit what it had received, but none could create courage.

Nechemiah stood beside the open ledger. “You know the teaching about the messengers lowering their wings?”

Avram did. He had heard it as a boy and never understood why heavenly love and awe should yield before the clumsy devotion of embodied people.

“Because what rises from below can call forth a light that was not previously theirs,” Nechemiah said. “A man trembles in a body, with debts, heat, hunger, pride, and distraction. If from there he turns truly upward, the response is not merely his small feeling enlarged. Something new is drawn. When that new light passes through the higher messengers, their former flight is no longer the measure.”

Avram looked at Leah’s linen strip. It was stained where she had wiped a child’s mouth.

He took the blue flag himself.

At the first signal pole he raised it over the red. Then he ordered Uri to run ahead with a new command: half-flow to the lower quarter immediately; the upper gardens would close until the cracked cistern was repaired. Avram’s own household terraces would receive no water that day.

The order moved downhill. Gate after gate reopened.

At Eliakim’s kiln, the foreman protested that the blue signal contradicted the sequence above him. Uri answered that the steward had issued a new instruction precisely because the old sequence had failed. Water entered the lower channel with a violent cough, throwing brown air from the pipe. Mattan knelt beside the measuring notch until the flow steadied at half-height.

Leah filled her jars first for the clinic and then handed the remaining place in line to a tannery widow whose cistern had gone dry.

By afternoon the signal poles stood blue from hilltop to market. Their flags drooped in the windless heat. Nechemiah, arriving slowly behind everyone else, looked up at them.

“Today the messengers lowered their wings,” he said.

Avram almost smiled, then noticed how pleased he was with himself and stopped. The danger was immediate: even a new light could become property in the mind of the one through whom it passed.

Mattan read his expression. “The water is not praising the gate.”

“No,” Avram said.

It was the first time he answered without defense.

That evening he amended the rulebook. Any gatekeeper could reduce pressure for danger, but only the spring steward together with one lower-quarter witness could close the final descent. He wrote Mattan’s name into the witness line.

A new instruction had passed through the old chain and changed it. The poles remained the same wood, the gates the same stone, yet the town had received something its previous arrangement could not produce by itself.
