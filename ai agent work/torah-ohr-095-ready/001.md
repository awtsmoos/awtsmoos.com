# The Cup That Had to Grow

At the end of the dry month, every public cistern in Giv’at Or carried a white ring three handbreadths below its usual line. The mark frightened the lower quarter first. Potters could work with less water, but not with none; healers could stretch wine, oil, and linen, but not wash fever from a child with dust. Above them, behind the spring house, Avram son of Netanel still had water moving through stone channels into shaded gardens.

Avram was not a miser. That was what made the dispute difficult. He had paid for widows’ jars in winter, forgiven irrigation debts after hail, and ordered his servants to leave a copper dipper at the roadside basin so that no traveler had to beg. When Mattan climbed from the lower quarter with a survey board under his arm and asked to cut a new feeder channel toward the clay yards, Avram answered by doubling the next day’s ration instead.

“More water,” Avram said, “is the answer to thirst.”

“Sometimes,” Mattan replied, “the vessel is the answer.”

Avram frowned. Mattan son of Azriel was a potter, not a trustee. He smelled of kiln smoke and wet earth, and his hands always looked as though they had just emerged from the ground.

Before the argument could harden, Leah came through the spring gate carrying an infant wrapped in blue wool. The child’s mother followed, exhausted from three nights of fever. Leah daughter of Shimon served as healer, midwife, and wet nurse when necessity demanded all three. She set the infant in Avram’s wife’s shade and asked for warm goat’s milk.

“The child needs filling?” Avram asked, still looking at Mattan.

“Not filling.” Leah tested the milk against her wrist. “Growing.”

She explained while the infant drank. A newborn did not simply receive food into an unchanged container. Milk became blood, flesh, strength, and the enlargement of organs themselves. The nourishment enabled the body to become capable of receiving more nourishment. Growth was not a larger heap inside the same cup; the cup itself changed.

Nechemiah, the old teacher who had come for the noon measure, heard her and smiled. He pointed toward the upper spring. “Our fathers used water for kindness because water makes growth possible. Abraham opened what was closed. But the teaching is finer than generosity alone. When light increases, vessels must enlarge. חסד שבחסד, גבורה שבחסד—kindness itself acquires structure, measure, and new capacity.”

Avram disliked lessons that arrived disguised as engineering criticism. “The upper reservoir can hold another ten thousand jars.”

“And the lower quarter?” Mattan asked.

“It can receive from the reservoir.”

“Through one old pipe that narrows behind Eliakim’s kiln.”

The infant coughed, then settled against Leah’s shoulder. In that tiny movement Avram saw something he could not dismiss: life had entered, and the receiver had been altered by receiving it.

That afternoon he authorized stone for a larger upper basin. Mattan signed the order because more storage would help. But beneath his signature he drew, without permission, a second line: a proposed channel descending directly toward the potters’ yards.

Avram crossed it out.

The water continued to fall from the upper spring with the same clear sound it had made before their disagreement. Yet by evening, all three of them understood that Giv’at Or’s crisis was no longer only how much blessing the town possessed. It was whether the town’s vessels would be enlarged enough for blessing to reach where it had never been allowed to go.
