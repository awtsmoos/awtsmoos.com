# Five Voices, Two Directions

Caleb counted the Sinai language in Seth’s scroll and asked why Scripture used the plural. If the revelation was one, why speak of voices?

Haggai answered from the source: Torah was given in five voices, and those voices are associated with five drawings of light, like five waters descending. Water remains the image of kindness and downward extension, carrying revelation from above toward below.

Uri said, “And the torches?”

“Fire. The opposite movement—upward burning, *ratzo*.”

Haggai set five small cups in a row and poured water from one pitcher into them. Then he lit a brazier several paces away. “Do not count these cups as mystical powers,” he warned Nadav before Nadav could begin. “They only let us keep the directions visible: water descends; flame rises.”

The source places both at Sinai: voices and torches, drawing downward and yearning upward. Their coexistence returns to the same question as fire and water. What prevents one from canceling the other?

“The shofar,” Baruch said.

“More precisely, the source to which the shofar points. Because both movements emerge from what is higher than their opposition, the Torah can contain both.”

Nadav looked at the five cups. “So five voices mean five separate gods?”

Haggai’s expression hardened. “No. Five drawings, not five sources. The teaching is precisely about multiple revelations proceeding from the One.”

At sunset the cups reflected the sky while the brazier sent sparks upward. Nothing miraculous happened in the yard. Yet the workers could now hear the plural at Sinai without imagining division. Many voices could draw; torches could rise; and the simple shofar stood before them as the sign of a source in which the many had not yet become separate.
