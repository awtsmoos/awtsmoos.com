# Forty Days Above the Storehouse

A caravan was delayed by snow, and Tal-Or counted every sack of grain. Nadav used the shortage to argue that no one, however holy, could rise above food for long.

Haggai answered with the source’s example of Moses on the mountain. For forty days he neither ate bread nor drank water—not even the manna that later sustained Israel in the wilderness. His receiving of Torah belonged to a level above the ordinary nourishment of the world.

Uri asked, “Why mention the manna? It was already bread from heaven.”

“Because the teaching is measuring the height of the giving. Even a refined food would still be food. Moses stood there to receive Torah at a level the angels themselves desired.”

Haggai recalled their plea: “Place Your glory upon the heavens.” The heavenly beings wanted Torah to remain above. The answer to them would reveal the purpose of bringing it into physical commandments below.

Nadav folded his arms. “Then the angels were right that Torah was higher.”

“They were right about its height, not about where it was meant to be given.”

The distinction silenced the room. Tal-Or had spent weeks learning that physical mitzvot could draw what spiritual ascent alone could not distribute to every person. Now Moses’ fasting sharpened the paradox: the Torah descends into bodily action precisely though its root is above the level where bodily food is needed.

That evening the caravan finally appeared in the pass. Uri opened the storehouse and began measuring grain. Haggai ate with the others. No one tried to imitate Moses’ forty days.

But as the bowls were passed, Seth looked toward the snowy ridge and understood why the angels’ question mattered. Torah did not descend because it belonged naturally to hunger and clay. It descended from a height that could have seemed to justify keeping it in heaven.
