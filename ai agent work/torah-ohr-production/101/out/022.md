# When the Wings Went Still

Before dawn Haggai took the apprentices outside the settlement, where wind moved through a stand of reeds. Their leaves beat and shivered so loudly that Nadav said the whole field sounded restless.

“That restlessness will help with the prayer-image in the source,” Haggai said.

He described the blessings before the Shema, where the worship of angels and *ofanim* is spoken of with tremendous excitement. Their cry and movement express *ratzo*, the upward surge of creatures before the Divine. The teaching compares their wings and movement with the lower side of an icy firmament: beneath it, the creatures burn and rush; above it lies a higher source.

“And the shofar?” Seth asked.

“Above that division. The shofar points to the source from which both running and returning can be included.”

Haggai then moved them to the central prayer, the Amidah. “Its name is standing. The prophetic creatures are described as ‘when they stood, they let down their wings.’ In the higher standing, the separate rush of *ratzo* and separate return of *shov* are quieted into a deeper unity.”

Nadav whispered, “So stillness is higher than passion?”

“Do not turn it into another contest. The source is not praising lifelessness. It is showing a state where the two movements no longer insist on themselves as separate.”

The wind dropped suddenly. Reeds that had rattled a moment earlier stood nearly motionless. No one claimed nature had arranged itself to prove the lesson. Yet the silence gave them an image they could carry back to prayer.

In Tal-Or that morning, men who had argued, hurried, feared, and longed stood with their feet together. Haggai heard no shofar. He needed none. The lesson had reached the place called standing, where even wings could rest because the source above their motion held both directions at once.
