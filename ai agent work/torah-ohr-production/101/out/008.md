# A Road for Ordinary Feet

Baruch could sit through Haggai’s longest teachings and follow the movement from longing to return. Uri could sometimes do the same before dawn, when the ovens were quiet. Most of Tal-Or could not. Children cried, carts broke, bread burned, shifts changed.

Nadav used that fact as an excuse. “If this running and returning belongs to Abraham and to angels, then leave it to people built for it.”

Haggai shook his head. “That is exactly where the teaching turns toward the giving of the Torah.”

He gathered the settlement in the yard and gave them no meditation. Instead he gave concrete tasks: return a borrowed tool, weigh a ration honestly, repair a widow’s roof before decorating the council hall. These were not presented as new commandments; they were ordinary Tal-Or exercises meant to make one point visible. Action reaches people whom lofty contemplation may not.

“The spiritual levels we described are indeed exceedingly high,” Haggai said. “If the whole path depended on sustaining such awareness, it would remain the province of the righteous and the heavenly beings. The Torah was given below in physical commandments so that every Israelite could enter the movement through actual deed.”

Seth asked, “The same *ratzo* and *shov*?”

“The source will show us how. But yes—the giving of Torah brings the road into physical action.”

Nadav looked dissatisfied. A mystical privilege would have let him disqualify the baker, the porter, and the child. A deed placed the question at his own door.

That evening the settlement repaired a broken water channel. No one mistook the ditch for Sinai. Yet hands that could not hold a vast contemplation still lifted stones, set them in their exact places, and carried water toward homes that needed it.

Haggai watched the channel begin to flow. “A road only for angels is not yet the road the Torah came down to make.”
