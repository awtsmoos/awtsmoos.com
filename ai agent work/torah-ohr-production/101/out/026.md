# Those Who Saw, Those Who Only Moved

At the gate Caleb demonstrated two kinds of warning. First he raised a torch where everyone could see it. Then he struck a hidden gong beyond the wall. Men reacted to both, but not in the same way.

Haggai used the difference to open the source’s distinction at Sinai. The mountain’s smoke belonged with the holy movements already described—fire taking hold of physicality so that *ratzo* and *shov* could enter the lower world. Yet Scripture divides the people present into two descriptions.

Of Israel it says, “All the people saw the voices.” Later it says, “The people saw and moved, and stood afar.” The source identifies the latter reaction with the mixed multitude, the *Erev Rav*. They were shaken by what happened, but they did not ‘see the voices’ in the inward sense attributed to Israel.

Nadav asked, “How can anyone see a voice?”

“That phrase is itself part of the teaching. The voices are Divine drawings. To ‘see’ them here means more than noticing spectacle. Israel’s souls perceived the revelation in a way that produced inward nullification.”

“And the mixed multitude?”

“They moved. They were affected. But the source says they stood from afar.”

Haggai would not let Tal-Or assign living neighbors to either category. “This is a reading of Sinai, not a license for you to judge another man’s soul.”

Caleb lowered the torch. Beyond the wall the gong sounded once more. Everyone turned toward it, yet Haggai’s distinction remained: reaction alone did not tell what had been perceived. At Sinai, according to the source, some saw the Divine voices; others trembled before the event and still remained distant.
