# Fire Went Up and Water Came Down

When Seth finished copying the new charter, Nadav asked the question he had saved for a crowded room. “If righteous men lived before Sinai, why did the mountain matter? Was Abraham lacking a path?”

Haggai drew two marks on the quarry wall: an arrow rising and an arrow returning. “Abraham was not lacking. The teaching says he fulfilled Torah in its spiritual root. His life was a continual journey—going and traveling southward—until he became a chariot, entirely given over to the Divine will.”

Baruch pointed toward the smelting yard, where flame climbed from the furnace while a water channel ran down toward the cooling trough.

“Use those,” Haggai said. “Fire rises. Water descends. The soul also knows an upward movement and a returning movement.”

He explained the upward motion as thirst: the heart perceives that created life cannot contain the Infinite and longs to leave its own limits. The returning motion is not betrayal of that longing. It is the other half of service, the return from yearning into the place where the Divine purpose is to be carried out.

Nadav frowned. “Then what was new at Sinai?”

“That is the question the teaching is opening. Before we answer it fully, understand first how high Abraham’s service already was. Sinai was not required because no one had ever ascended.”

At that moment a furnace worker shouted. The ore had reached heat, but without the cooling channel the metal would be ruined. Baruch opened the sluice. Steam rose as water met the work of fire.

“Higher peace,” Haggai said, “can join what seems opposed. Even above, fire and water do not destroy one another when they are included in a greater purpose.”

Seth added nothing to the charter. The lesson was not yet the answer. It was the measure of the question: if Abraham had already mastered the road of rising and returning, Sinai’s novelty had to lie somewhere deeper than spiritual greatness alone.
