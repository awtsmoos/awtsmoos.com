# The King Was Not the Lamplight

Tal-Or’s council chamber was lit by a single polished lamp. Nadav, pleased with the new bronze reflector, told the workers that the lamp now “filled the whole hall.”

Haggai heard him and smiled. “Useful words for a lamp. Dangerous words if you forget what they mean when speaking of the Holy One.”

He asked Seth to bring the teaching they had copied about kingship. Worlds, Haggai explained, receive from Divine kingship; even the life called filling worlds and the life called surrounding worlds are not the Essence Himself. The worlds live from a radiance of His kingship. He is holy—separate, beyond the measures by which worlds receive.

Nadav pointed at the reflector. “Then this is a good image. The light reaches the walls while the lamp remains here.”

“Only if you remember where the image breaks,” Haggai said. “No physical lamp can contain what is beyond all worlds. The comparison is only to keep you from confusing a ray with its source.”

Baruch stood at the doorway. “And what does a soul do when it understands that?”

“It longs not merely for a brighter ray. It longs to be nullified in the Infinite Himself.”

That answer disturbed Nadav more than any rule about grain. He liked improvement: more lamps, more ore, more rooms, more authority. A longing that did not end in possessing a larger thing gave him nothing to count.

That night the reflector cracked from heat. The hall darkened at once, and Nadav cursed the loss as if authority itself had vanished.

Haggai relit a smaller wick. “The radiance can be less or more to us,” he said. “Do not therefore imagine that the Source has become less or more.”

The council finished its work under weak light. Outside, the mountains were black. No one could see beyond them, and Haggai made no attempt to draw the Infinite as another object on the far side. The lesson was precisely that He was not another object at all.
