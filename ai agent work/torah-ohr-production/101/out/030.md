# The Terror of What Has No Place

During a mine inspection, Nadav became frightened in a chamber so large that his lamp could not reach the far wall. “Now I understand the angels,” he said. “They fear because heaven is enormous.”

Haggai shook his head. “The source says almost the opposite.”

Holy angels possess tremendous fear and bittul because the Infinite Himself is revealed to them. Their fear is not produced by measuring a larger room, a larger star, or even a larger heaven. It comes from revelation of what is not contained by place at all.

By contrast, the forces of *kelipah* receive from a separated order in which beings stand as self and place. Because that separation is maintained, the revelation of infinity does not shine there in the same way. They can call Him ‘God of gods’—acknowledging a supreme power over other powers—while still imagining a world of separate domains beneath Him.

Nadav lifted his lamp. “So saying He is stronger than every other power is still not the whole truth?”

“Correct. Infinity is not merely the winning power in a contest among powers.”

Baruch marked the chamber wall so the miners would not lose their route. In the mine, place mattered desperately: here was the shaft, there the unstable seam, beyond it the exit. Haggai honored those boundaries. The lesson concerned the Divine, not tunnel safety.

When they emerged, the sky had no visible walls, yet even the sky was still created expanse. Haggai repeated the distinction: holiness trembles before revelation of the Infinite Himself; separation can fear authority while preserving its own imagined place.

Nadav’s lamp had failed to reach the wall. That was only darkness. The Infinite was not a farther wall his light could not find.
