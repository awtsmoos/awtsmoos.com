# Bread from the Higher Ruin

Nadav arrived early to Haggai’s lesson with a challenge prepared. “If man also comes from Divine speech, why must he borrow life from the speech within bread?”

Haggai nodded. “Now the source answers more deeply. It is not that food alone has a Divine utterance. Man does as well. The distinction concerns root.”

He drew a vessel on a shard of pottery and struck it with a stone. The pieces scattered across the floor. “The teaching speaks of the shattering of the vessels. Sparks from a high source fell downward into the realm of *kelipat nogah*. Food, though low in its present form, can therefore contain a root higher than the human level that now receives from it.”

Uri watched Haggai gather the fragments. “Higher things fell farther?”

“That is the structure being taught. The present coarseness does not tell you the original height.”

Baruch compared it to rich ore hidden beneath worthless stone, then immediately corrected himself: “Only an illustration. Ore never fell from a spiritual world.”

Haggai smiled. “Good. Keep the boundary.”

The matter also explained why eating is not merely a humiliating dependency. A person can receive vitality from food because the Divine life in that food has a root capable of nourishing him. But the same fall that places the spark below also clothes it in mixture and coarseness.

Nadav stared at the broken pottery. He had always ranked things by where they stood now: council above baker, man above grain, polished metal above slag.

Uri swept the shards into a basket. The loaf beside him remained ordinary bread, not a relic of shattered worlds. Yet the source had taught them to see its paradox: low enough to be chewed, rooted high enough to give life.
