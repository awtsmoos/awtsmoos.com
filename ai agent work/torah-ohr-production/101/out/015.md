# The Meal That Pulled Downward

For a week Uri served the miners richer food than usual because a trader had paid his debt in oil and dried fruit. Work slowed. Men lingered after meals. Nadav began ordering second portions for himself and calling them compensation for administration.

Haggai did not condemn eating. He used the moment to explain the next difficulty in the source. Food’s higher root does not mean its present material form is pure. After the fall into *kelipat nogah*, food is coarse enough to pull the eater toward material desire.

Uri looked embarrassed. “Then nourishment contains both a higher root and a lower danger.”

“Exactly. The root explains how it can sustain. The fall explains why eating can drag a person after the world.”

Haggai added a sharper point from the teaching: even a righteous person who eats entirely for the right purpose would, from the standpoint of material dependence itself, be better if the body did not require eating. Holiness does not mean pretending that physical need is already the highest state.

Nadav pushed away his extra bowl. “So a noble intention does not make appetite itself holy?”

“Intention can direct the act. It does not erase the coarseness of the material condition.”

The next day Uri restored ordinary portions. Baruch ate, blessed, and returned to the shaft instead of praising hunger or indulgence. Haggai wanted neither mistake. The source had not called food evil; it had called attention to the mixed condition into which its vitality had fallen.

At sunset Nadav passed the bakery and smelled fresh loaves. He stopped, felt the pull, and kept walking until the proper meal. For once he did not call restraint deprivation. He had learned that a thing could carry a high root and still, in its fallen clothing, tug the soul downward.
