# Hide, Flour, and the Great Question

Uri spread three things on the worktable: a sack of flour, a strip of cured hide, and a length of wool. “These are ordinary,” he said. “How can ordinary matter carry what you have described?”

Haggai said that the question was the teaching’s own. The materials of mitzvot come from the physical kingdoms of growth and animal life, sustained under *kelipat nogah*: flour for a meal-offering, animal life for offerings, hide for tefillin. Yet through a mitzvah these very materials can become instruments for drawing the Infinite below.

Nadav pinched flour between two fingers. “Then the holiness was hiding in the grain all along?”

“Do not make the answer before the source makes it,” Haggai warned. “We are first establishing the wonder. The material is physical; its ordinary vitality belongs to the mixed realm. Nevertheless Torah after Sinai takes precisely such things and makes them vehicles of command.”

Baruch lifted the hide. It had once belonged to an animal that knew nothing of letters, law, or prayer. “And this can become tefillin?”

“That is exactly the force of the question.”

Tal-Or’s craftsmen were used to refining crude things: ore into metal, grain into bread, hide into parchment. But Haggai refused to let craftsmanship explain the entire matter. Human skill could change usefulness; it could not by itself explain why physical substance should draw a light beyond worlds.

Uri tied the flour sack shut again. The table looked no different than before. Yet the teaching had shifted the burden of explanation. Sinai’s novelty was not an escape from matter. It was that matter itself—low, mixed, ordinary matter—could now be taken into a commandment and made to serve a purpose far above its apparent place.
