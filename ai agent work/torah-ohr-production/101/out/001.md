# The Sound Before the Order

The watchmen of Tal-Or had three signals: one for fire, one for a cave-in, and one for raiders. Everyone knew the pattern of each. Yet when Caleb found an old ram’s horn among the storehouse tools and sounded one long plain note across the valley, men dropped baskets before knowing why.

Nadav came running from the ration shed. “What happened?”

“Nothing,” Caleb said. “I only tested the horn.”

Nadav was angry because his hands were shaking. “Then use a bell. Bells tell a man what to do.”

Haggai, who had been teaching beside the quarry wall, heard him. “That is exactly the difference. An order waits for the mind. A simple blast can seize a man before the mind has arranged its reasons.”

He reminded them of the verse they had learned: if a shofar is sounded in a city, will the people not tremble? At Sinai there were voices and flames, but Scripture also names the voice of the shofar. Its plainness was part of its force. It did not need melody or explanation in order to cast awe.

That afternoon Haggai asked Caleb to sound the horn again without warning. Uri, kneading dough at the bakery table, stopped with both hands buried in flour. Baruch looked up from a pan of ore. Even Nadav, who had prepared himself to remain unmoved, turned toward the ridge.

“No command has been spoken,” Haggai said. “Still, something in you has already answered.”

Nadav tried to laugh, but the sound would not come.

Caleb hung the horn by the public gate. It was not given a signal-code that day. For once Tal-Or kept an instrument whose first lesson was not information, but trembling.
