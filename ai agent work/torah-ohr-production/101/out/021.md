# From the Narrow Mouth to the Wide Hall

Caleb held the shofar toward the morning light. Its mouth was narrow at one end and broad at the other. Nadav had seen the horn every day for weeks and never thought its shape mattered.

Haggai quoted the verse: “From the narrow place I called to God; He answered me with breadth.”

“The source uses the shofar itself to teach this movement,” he said. “The blast begins from the narrow place and comes out into breadth.”

Seth asked whether the narrowness meant distress.

“It includes the verse’s language of *meitzar*, but the teaching carries us higher than ordinary distress. The narrow source is beyond grasp, beyond the spread of understanding. It is the hidden source of delight from which the wider revelations emerge.”

Haggai traced the horn without blowing it. From that concealed source, the teaching speaks of the upper yud of wisdom and the upper heh of understanding—the point and its breadth. Then, in the lower order of service, waterlike wisdom brings bittul and the heart can blaze with fire and thirst.

Nadav frowned. “You are putting opposites back together again.”

“That is why we came to the shofar. Water and fire, bittul and longing, narrow point and expansion: they are not patched together afterward. Their roots emerge from one source higher than their difference.”

Caleb sounded a single note. The breath entered where the horn was tight and filled the valley from where it opened wide.

Haggai would not let the acoustics become proof. A ram’s horn was still a ram’s horn. But its form carried the source’s chosen image: from concealment beyond comprehension came the powers that later appeared as distinct and even opposed.

Nadav looked from the narrow mouth to the broad bell. For the first time the simple note did not seem simple because it lacked content. It seemed simple because it came from before the divisions.
