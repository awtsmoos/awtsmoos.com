# Where the Voice Could Rest

Seth asked why a Divine voice should be revealed in one place and concealed in another if the Infinite has no limit. Haggai answered that the source now turns from the voice to the listener.

“The drawing of the Infinite rests where there is *bittul*,” he said. “Not because the Infinite is absent elsewhere, but because revealed infinity is received where selfhood does not stand as an obstruction.”

Nadav immediately tried to imitate humility by lowering his eyes and speaking softly. Haggai stopped him. “Posture can be useful. It is not the thing itself.”

The source connects Israel’s seeing of the voices with an inward response of the soul. When a person is touched by what exceeds his own measure, the heart does not merely collect information. The self yields. That yielded place is capable of receiving the drawing called voice.

Baruch compared it to a vessel emptied before it is filled. Haggai accepted the comparison only halfway. “A physical vessel must be empty because space is limited. Divine revelation is not competing for space. The point is that self-assertion hides what bittul reveals.”

That day Nadav was asked to settle a dispute over stored grain. He began by preparing a speech defending his old policy. Then he listened to Uri’s accounts and discovered that he had misread the numbers. For once he crossed out his own argument before anyone forced him.

Haggai did not call the correction a mystical revelation. It was simply a small human instance of surrendering a position when truth displaced it.

At the evening lesson Seth read again: “All the people saw the voices.” Haggai let the phrase stand. Voice was a Divine drawing; seeing it belonged to souls made receptive through inward nullification. The place where the voice could rest was not marked by walls. It was marked by the disappearance of the claim, “I stand here on my own.”
