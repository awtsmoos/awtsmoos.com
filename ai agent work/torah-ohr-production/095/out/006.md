# The Cart That Did Not Block the Sky

On tithe day, carts from the fields crowded Tal-Or’s lower court. Sacks of grain, jars of oil, and baskets of fruit waited to be measured for the portions set aside by law. Nadav found the work offensively ordinary.

“If the teaching speaks of the Infinite,” he told Haggai, “why bring it down to barley?”

“Because that is exactly where Moshe’s drawing reaches,” Haggai said.

He spoke of the quality called Mah: bittul, the surrender of self-importance before the Divine. Through that nullification, the teaching says, Moshe drew the light of Ein Sof even into Asiyah and into practical commandments—terumot, maasrot, acts performed with physical produce.

Nadav kicked dust from his sandal. “The world is still here. The sacks are still sacks.”

Haggai nodded. Bittul did not require pretending that grain had become air. It meant that the existence of the lower world did not constitute an interruption before the Divine. From one perspective creation appears as something; from the higher perspective it occupies no independent place. The source calls this the two “knowledges” of the verse concerning the God of knowledges.

A wagon wheel squealed nearby. Haggai pointed to it. “The teaching also uses the image of a wheel within a wheel. What is higher can encompass what is lower and reach farther down. In the world of Asiyah this becomes vivid in the Ofanim.”

Baruch spent the morning lifting sacks onto the scales. He did not speak during the lesson. Each time he set aside the required portion, he did it without carving his name into the tally board. Seth noticed and began removing the clerk’s decorative marks he had once added beside his own entries.

At midday Haggai cited Rav Yehuda, whose awe of his Master was so present that the sages spoke of him as beyond the ordinary measure of a man born of woman, and the passage concludes that therefore he was exempt from prayer. Nadav wanted to turn the story into status. Haggai turned it back toward bittul.

The point was not escape from the physical. The carts remained, the scales remained, and the law was fulfilled through them. The world did not have to disappear for it to cease acting as a barrier.

When the final basket was measured, a shaft of afternoon light crossed the dusty court and struck the brass scale. Nadav moved the cart that blocked the gate, then stopped.

For years he had imagined holiness as something whose path the physical world obstructed. Now the source asked for a harder surrender: not to deny the world, but to deny its claim to stand as an independent wall before the Infinite. The barley stayed heavy in his hands. It simply no longer had to block the sky.
