# The Answering Court

Tal-Or’s council met in the lower hall, the room where taxes, seed, wages, and disputes were turned into enforceable decisions. Haggai called it the settlement’s chamber of rule.

That morning he taught the phrase, “And Elokim will answer him.”

In the source, he explained, the name Elokim is read here in the sense of authority and governance, corresponding to Malchut in Beriah, Yetzirah, and Asiyah. Torah is drawn into that realm of rule. The tractate of Zeraim, dealing with seeds, is given as an example of chesed of the feminine aspect.

Nadav looked around at the benches. “Then our council is Malchut?”

“No,” Haggai said immediately. “This room is only a parable for reception and rule. Do not turn a correspondence into an identity.”

The case before them concerned seed grain. Three families had lost part of their allotment to damp storage, and the rules did not specify whether replacement seed should come from the common reserve. Nadav read the written clause twice and insisted that silence meant refusal.

Seth asked Haggai about the other word in the verse: “will answer.”

Haggai cited the sages’ use of answering as repeating after those who speak. The lower level does not invent the Torah it receives; it answers it, responding to the voice drawn into it.

The council returned to the charter’s principles and asked what the seed law was for. The reserve existed so fields would be planted. If families lacked seed because of negligence, the matter would be different; here the damp had entered through a cracked public roof.

Nadav still disliked expanding the rule, but he could no longer pretend that authority meant merely enforcing whatever sentence lay closest at hand. Rule had to become an answering vessel.

They granted replacement seed and ordered the storehouse roof repaired.

Outside, Uri helped distribute the grain. He poured each family’s measure into cloth sacks while Haggai watched the phrase from the lesson become a visible sequence: teaching descending toward governance, governance answering by giving the teaching a form in the lower place.

No mystical claim depended on Tal-Or’s verdict. The source’s claim stood on its own. Yet the council now possessed a concrete analogy: Malchut as the sphere where what descends is received as rule, and “answering” as a response that echoes what was spoken into it.

By noon, seed that had existed only as a disputed entry in the ledger was moving toward actual fields.
