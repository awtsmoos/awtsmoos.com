# The Mercy Kept Before It Flowed

Behind Tal-Or’s bathhouse lay a spring no one saw directly. Water gathered first in a covered stone chamber, then entered a narrow channel and finally emerged through public spouts. Nadav called the hidden chamber wasteful. If water was meant for use, he argued, it should be visible at once.

Haggai brought him there while teaching the passage about Moses.

“All forty-nine gates of understanding were given to Moses,” he said, “and therefore he stood near the fiftieth gate and could draw from it.”

He spoke of the thirteen attributes of mercy and of the Zohar’s reading that Moses called and drew the Divine Name. These attributes, the passage said, were sources for the unfolding of the worlds.

Then Haggai wrote one word on the damp wall: *notzer*—“preserving” or “guarding,” as in *notzer chesed*.

He rearranged its Hebrew letters to show the associations named in the teaching: *tzinor*, a channel, and *ratzon*, will. Yet *notzer* also meant something kept—guarded in thought, concealed before it came into revelation.

Nadav tapped the covered reservoir. “So concealment is holy now?”

“Not every hiding,” Haggai said. “You hid bread so others could not receive it. This chamber holds water so that it can be given in ordered flow. The passage distinguishes a kindness still at its supernal source from a kindness already expressed within the order of worlds.”

He contrasted *notzer chesed* with *oseh chesed*. The latter, “doing kindness,” belonged to the level associated with Abraham, chesed of Atzilut, a kindness already within the ordered unfolding. *Notzer chesed* was spoken of as far above that order, a root and source before the later stages.

That difference, Haggai explained, also illuminated the placement of the phrases among the thirteen attributes. *Notzer chesed* appears before the phrase concerning visiting ancestral iniquity and before the close of the attributes with *venakeh*; *oseh chesed*, in its other scriptural setting, appears afterward because it belongs to kindness within the chain of ordered expression.

Uri arrived carrying a cracked clay pipe. “If the source stays hidden forever,” he said, “the village still thirsts.”

“Correct,” Haggai answered. “Guarded is not abandoned. A channel exists because what is concealed is meant to become revelation.”

The practical question came sooner than expected. Fever spread through the western huts, and the bathhouse cistern ran low. Nadav wanted to open the spring chamber completely. Baruch warned that the sudden pressure would collapse the old conduit.

Instead they repaired the narrow channel and released the stored water at a rate the pipes could bear. The hidden reserve became public mercy precisely through measure.

Nadav watched the first clear stream fill Uri’s jars. “Then Moses is the channel?”

Haggai answered with the limits of the text. “The passage associates Moses with drawing from that higher source. It plays on *notzer* as channel and will, and as preservation in concealment. Do not flatten all of that into plumbing.”

That evening Seth entered the spring in the public ledger for the first time. He did not expose the chamber’s exact location, which remained protected against contamination. Instead he recorded its purpose, its guardians, and the law that its water belonged to the settlement.

Something could be hidden without being stolen. Something could be guarded precisely so it might one day flow.
