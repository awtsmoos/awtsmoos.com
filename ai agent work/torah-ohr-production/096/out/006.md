# A Hundred Fasts Before the Higher Lesson

Seth had become proud of forgetting the old ration system. He crossed out Nadav’s secret abbreviations, rewrote the categories, and announced that Tal-Or had entered a cleaner age.

Haggai listened, then asked him to bring the Babylonian folio from the study chest.

“The passage gives a sharper example,” he said. “When one rises from the lower Gan Eden to the upper, there is again a River Dinur. Not only bodily memory must be left behind. Even the understanding and delight of the lower Gan Eden must be forgotten for the higher ascent.”

Seth stopped smiling.

Haggai then cited the Talmudic example named by the Torah Ohr: Rabbi Zeira fasted one hundred fasts so that he would forget the Babylonian Talmud. The point was not that his Babylonian learning had been poor. The passage stresses the opposite—he had learned it with extraordinary clarity, and it alludes to the account of fire that did not rule over him, known from the story of the scorched leg.

Uri leaned over the table. “So the obstacle can be something true?”

“Something true at its level,” Haggai said. “The teaching speaks of ascent after ascent. A prior attainment can become the thing that must no longer set the measure for what comes next.”

Seth looked at the new ledgers. He had rebuilt them using the same hidden assumption as Nadav: every person had to be reduced to a productivity score, only now the score was calculated more honestly. The numbers no longer lied, but they still defined need by output.

Baruch saw the realization before Seth spoke. “You cleaned the old vessel,” he said. “You did not ask whether every later account had to use it.”

Seth was angry because the criticism was correct.

For three days he reviewed the distribution system with Uri and the widows’ council. They did not discard arithmetic. They changed what arithmetic served. Work capacity remained relevant to assigning mine shifts; it ceased to determine whether a sick child ate. The old categories had contained real knowledge, but that knowledge could not govern every higher obligation.

Nadav, unexpectedly, defended Seth. “A man should not be mocked for the ladder he used to climb.”

Haggai nodded. “Nor should he carry the ladder on his back after reaching the roof.”

Again he guarded the source from careless moralizing. Rabbi Zeira’s fasts were not a management technique, and the River Dinur was not a metaphor invented by Tal-Or. The Torah Ohr had brought both as evidence that transition between levels requires a forgetting proportionate to the new level, even when the former attainment was precious.

At the next public assembly Seth presented two ledgers. One tracked labor. The other tracked provisions owed to persons as persons. The columns no longer pretended to be one science.

He kept the old book in the archive, not on the ration desk.

That night he asked Haggai, “How do I know when I have reached a level that requires another forgetting?”

Haggai closed the folio. “The passage has not yet finished. First learn how many levels there are.”
