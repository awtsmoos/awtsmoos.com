# The River That Took the Old Memory

The winter after the flood, old Menachem the assayer died. For thirty years he had known every seam in Tal-Or’s mine by touch. At the mourning house, miners repeated his measurements as if reciting them might keep him near.

Nadav sat apart, listening. “If memory is loyalty,” he said to Haggai, “why does your teaching speak of forgetting?”

Haggai did not answer with a story about Menachem’s soul. He opened the Torah Ohr and stayed within its words.

“On the two forms of kindness just discussed,” he said, “the verse says *la’alafim*—to thousands. The passage explains this as thousands of levels, one degree after another.”

He then described its example. After this physical world comes the lower Gan Eden. Between the conditions stands the River Dinur. One must pass through it so that the power of physical memory is forgotten, because while the mind remains held by corporeality it cannot delight in Gan Eden.

Uri looked toward Menachem’s empty chair. “Does that mean love must be erased?”

“The text does not say that,” Haggai replied. “It speaks about the forgetting of material apprehension as a condition for a different delight. We should not invent beyond it.”

Seth appreciated the restraint. Nadav did not. “A man is the sum of what he keeps,” he insisted.

For him the statement was almost literal. His private room still held fragments of old ledgers—debts paid years before, offenses already judged, names of men who had once opposed him. He called the papers memory. In truth he used them to keep every past relation available as leverage.

The next week the council ordered the obsolete ration records reviewed. Seth found entries that could no longer serve any lawful purpose. Nadav fought to preserve them all.

Haggai took the men to the ore-washing channel. Muddy concentrate entered one end; water carried away the clay so the heavier material could be seen. He warned them not to identify this wash with the River Dinur. It was only a local image of a more general truth: one state of perception could obstruct entry into another.

Nadav watched black water leave the trough. “And who decides what deserves forgetting?”

“For our ledgers, the council and the law,” Seth said. “For Gan Eden, do not make our bureaucracy the judge of heaven.”

They kept records required for restitution and destroyed those used only for private coercion. Nadav surrendered a bundle tied with red cord. Before placing it in the furnace he read every name once.

The fire curled the pages into ash. Nothing in the act pretended to be the River Dinur. No one claimed that burning paper created spiritual ascent. Yet Nadav felt the violence of releasing a form of knowledge that had become his identity.

At evening study Haggai returned to the word *la’alafim*.

“Do not imagine one transition only,” he said. “The teaching is preparing us for thousands of gradations.”

Seth closed the new ledger. Menachem’s name remained in the memorial register; the old ration threats did not. The settlement had not learned to despise memory. It had learned that a memory fitted to one world can become a wall before another.
