# The Face That Took No Side

Tal-Or’s rescue crews had argued for years about which entrance to the mine should command the others. The eastern crew painted a lion on its helmets. The western haulers marked their carts with an ox. Each group had saved lives; each had also learned to mistake its strength for the whole settlement.

After the storm, Haggai called both crews into the ore hall. On the wall he drew the forms named in the vision of the chariot. He marked the lion to the right and the ox to the left. Then he drew the human form without placing it on either side.

“The passage we are learning,” he said, “calls the one spirit the general life of the four living creatures. Their collectivity is the aspect of *Adam*.”

Baruch pointed to the lion. “So Adam is the middle between right and left?”

“Not merely a compromise,” Haggai replied. “The lion has a right; the ox has a left. The aspect called Adam is not defined by that division. For that reason Adam is named for resemblance to the Higher One—the Man upon the throne.”

Nadav immediately tried to use the teaching. The eastern crew, he argued, should surrender its independent stores to his office. A general authority, he said, must stand above sides.

Uri laughed without humor. “You hear ‘beyond right and left’ and somehow arrive at ‘inside Nadav’s lockbox.’”

The dispute might have remained words if a support beam had not cracked in the northern gallery that afternoon. Two miners were trapped beyond a fall. The eastern crew knew the ventilation shafts; the western haulers knew how to brace loose stone. Each captain insisted on leading.

Seth, now keeping the emergency ledger, wrote two columns—RIGHT and LEFT—and realized the page itself was preserving the quarrel.

Haggai took the charcoal from him and drew one heading across both columns: MEN BELOW.

The lion crew entered first through a narrow air passage and carried breathing tubes. The ox crew followed with timber and jacks. Neither skill could replace the other. When the first trapped miner emerged, soot covered the lion painted on one rescuer’s helmet and the ox on another’s coat. The marks remained, but the saving action had not belonged to one side.

Later, Haggai insisted that the lesson not be sentimentalized. The Torah Ohr had not said that right and left were unreal. It named them distinctly: lion on the right, ox on the left. What it revealed was a more inclusive life in which those distinctions were gathered into Adam.

Nadav sat at the council table with his hands spread over the old ration maps. “And the likeness to the One above?” he asked.

Haggai answered carefully. “The text says *Adam—adameh la’Elyon*, resembling the Higher, the Adam upon the throne. It is precisely this generality, not captivity to one side, that the name points toward.”

Seth recopied the rescue record. He kept the crew names, because truth required distinctions. But under both he entered the same outcome: *Two men returned alive.*

For the first time, he understood why a face could contain two eyes without becoming two persons.
