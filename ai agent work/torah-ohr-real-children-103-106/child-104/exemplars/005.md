# One Mark for Every Living Name

The charter scroll contained hundreds of letters, and Haggai told Tal-Or that each person bore responsibility for one portion no other could replace. Nadav mistrusted the teaching. It gave ordinary workers a dignity independent of his allocations.

He organized a census by assigning every resident a clay token. Councilors received stamped disks; miners received plain shards. Uri objected that the hierarchy contradicted the lesson. Nadav replied that all marks counted, though some deserved honor.

During distribution, Seth discovered two plain tokens missing from the register: an orphaned stable boy and an elderly ore sorter. Nadav had excluded them because neither household contributed full labor. Without tokens, they received no seed-bread.

Baruch broke his stamped foreman’s disk in half and gave each a piece. Others followed, making the count impossible. Nadav threatened punishment. Haggai asked whether the settlement existed for the register or the register for the souls.

They rebuilt the census publicly. Each person spoke his name, trade, dependency, and need. Uri’s sick daughter was entered without shame. The stable boy received a mark bearing a small horse. The ore sorter received one etched with a sieve. Distinct signs did not rank value; they preserved unrepeatable claims.

Nadav’s own token was changed from a crown to a measuring cup. He protested that office required recognition. The council answered that his responsibility, not grandeur, distinguished him.

That evening Seth found an old list of workers omitted from winter rations. Several had died or left Tal-Or. He recognized Nadav’s shorthand beside their names. Each missing mark concealed a life treated as excess.

The clerk copied the list and hid it beneath his mattress, not yet courageous enough to expose it.

At dawn, seed-bread was counted by names rather than political loyalty. The total seemed insufficient, but portions stretched because council families no longer received double.

Nadav watched the plain and decorated tokens mixed in one basket. The settlement’s world was drawn through many distinct letters. No single mark contained the whole charter, and no person could be erased without tearing its living text.
